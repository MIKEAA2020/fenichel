### 📊 Main Manuscript vs. Supplementary: A Comparison

Here is a practical breakdown of where typical manuscript elements usually belong.

| Element | Typically Main Manuscript | Typically Supplementary |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Figures & Tables** | The **key figures** (typically 4-8) that directly drive the narrative and support the central conclusion. | **Supporting figures/tables** that provide additional controls, replicate data, or validation but are not central to the main story. |
| **Methods** | A **concise overview** of the methods, sufficient for a reader to understand the general experimental design. | **Full, detailed protocols** and comprehensive descriptions of materials (e.g., primer lists, plasmid details, software algorithms) required for exact replication. |
| **Results & Discussion** | The **complete narrative** of results and discussion that directly supports the paper's main claims. | Generally **not permitted**. The SI should not contain new results or extended discussions that are separate from the main narrative. |
| **Data & Media** | Summarized data and the most critical, representative images or videos. | **Large datasets** (e.g., raw data tables, genomic sequences), **uncropped/raw images** (like full gels), and **non-printable media** (e.g., long videos, 3D models). |

### 🧭 How to Decide: Key Decision Criteria

When you are unsure about a specific piece of content, ask yourself these questions:

1.  **Is it Essential to the Core Conclusion?** If removing the item would leave a gap in the logical argument for your main finding, it belongs in the main text. If it merely adds support, nuance, or detail, it can go in the SI.
2.  **Is it for the General Reader or the Specialist?** The main text should be understandable to a broad audience in your field. If a figure or data table is only of interest to a small group of specialists, it is a strong candidate for the SI.
3.  **Is it a Matter of Practicality (Size or Format)?** If a figure is exceptionally large, a table is too long to fit on a journal page, or a video file cannot be embedded, it must go in the SI due to practical constraints.
4.  **Is it a "Tangential" or "Premature" Experiment?** Data that is interesting but not directly pertinent to the central focus of the study should be placed in the SI, or perhaps omitted entirely to avoid making the supplement a "dump" for less-reliable data.

### ⚠️ Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Be wary of these common traps when preparing your SI:

*   **The "Data Dump":** Do not use the SI to publish every experiment you have ever performed. It should be a curated collection of materials that directly enrich the paper, not a repository for tangential or low-quality data.
*   **Burying Key Findings:** Conversely, do not hide crucial data that is necessary for the main conclusion in the supplement. This can frustrate readers and reviewers who may feel the paper is incomplete.
*   **An Unlinked Supplement:** Every single item in the SI (e.g., "Supplementary Figure S1") **must be explicitly cited** in the main manuscript at a relevant point. The SI should not exist as a disconnected document.

Using the "essential vs. supportive" framework and prioritizing the clarity of your main narrative will guide you to the right choices.
